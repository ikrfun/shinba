# Evidence-led design review

Use these prompts when reviewing a design or helping a learner explore tradeoffs. Begin with the user's goal, scope, and known constraints. Inspect the relevant codebase and project instructions first: entry points and callers, business rules, persistence and external integrations, tests, dependency configuration, deployment manifests, timeouts, retries, resource limits, shutdown handling, and observability, where present. Follow the relevant paths, not just filenames. A missing file is not proof that a mechanism does not exist elsewhere. Record specific paths and line numbers for observations. Distinguish observed facts, assumptions, and unresolved questions; ask when deployment, infrastructure, workload, or business requirements are unavailable. Check current primary documentation for version- or provider-dependent behavior. Do not infer a Kubernetes deployment, a replica count, or a cloud service from a diagram alone.

Select only applicable questions and explain unfamiliar concepts with an example tied to the actual flow. Ask the learner for an expected outcome before presenting possible approaches. Include tradeoffs and the evidence needed to decide; do not claim every item is a defect or mandate a pattern. Link questions to the relevant card, flow, or case where possible. Proposed implementation or tests must be labeled as proposals until verified.

- **Repeated requests and idempotency:** For retries, duplicate submissions, messages, or restarts, what constitutes the same operation, within what scope and period? What results and side effects are acceptable? Inspect request identifiers, constraints, writes, and transaction boundaries. Consider simultaneous duplicates and partial completion, not only sequential repeats.
- **Retryability and failure:** Which failures are actually transient, and at which layer are retries configured? Inspect timeouts, retry limits, backoff, deadlines, side effects, and error propagation. What happens when a response is lost after the operation completes? Consider retry storms and recovery after exhausted retries where relevant.
- **Cost and resource use:** Which operations, stored data, transfers, requests, and compute resources drive cost for the actual design? What expected volume, file sizes, retention, and traffic patterns are known? Consider request amplification, storage lifecycle and data transfer where applicable. Do not invent prices or forecast spending without verified rates and workload assumptions.
- **Latency and throughput:** What end-to-end objective, typical and worst-case sizes, concurrency, and measurement are available? Identify serial steps, external calls, queues, timeouts, and long-running operations. Consider memory, CPU, and backpressure as supported by evidence; do not claim a bottleneck without measurement.
- **Multiple replicas and concurrent clients:** Only if the deployment or plausible requirements warrant it, inspect shared state, uniqueness constraints, locks, cache scope, ordering, duplicate messages, and distributed coordination. What happens when two instances process the same key concurrently? Do not assume a single-process lock coordinates multiple pods.
- **Shutdown and replacement:** If lifecycle and deployment information is available, inspect readiness, graceful termination, request draining, worker acknowledgement, cleanup, and deadlines. What happens to in-flight work during shutdown or rollout? Treat pod replacement as a relevant case only when a pod-based deployment is established or explicitly posed as a scenario.
- **Storage and partial failure:** Inspect ownership and transaction boundaries between storage, metadata, and response. Which artifacts remain when one step succeeds and another fails? What recovery, consistency, cleanup and authorization expectations apply? Verify external-service guarantees in official documentation.
- **Tests and visibility:** Map the selected cases to observable results and appropriate unit, integration, contract, or end-to-end tests. Include negative, concurrency, interruption and recovery paths where relevant; note real services versus test doubles. Identify logs, metrics, and tracing needed to diagnose failures without exposing sensitive data.

If information is missing, phrase a question or a conditional scenario and state what evidence would settle it. Never present an assumed configuration or hypothetical failure as an observed finding. Keep a concise rationale, source and tradeoff for each substantive review suggestion.
