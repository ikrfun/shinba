# Facilitation guide

Use only relevant prompts. Explain terms when needed, then ask for a concrete example in the system. Examples are starting points, not mandatory features.

- **Use cases:** Who wants what outcome? What triggers it, what must already be true, and what changes? Include alternatives, permissions, concurrent actions, and cancellation when relevant. Assign stable case IDs and expected results.
- **Information:** What facts enter, change, and leave the system? Who owns each fact? Define business terms, invariants, lifetimes, sensitive data, and unknowns. Distinguish domain concepts from API and persistence representations.
- **Sequence:** Trace actor, entry point, components, storage and external services in order. Show branches, transaction boundaries, asynchronous handoffs, retries and failure responses where applicable. Mermaid text is portable; validate diagrams in a compatible renderer when rendering matters.
- **Contract:** For each applicable operation, record method/path or equivalent entry point, authorization, inputs, required/optional fields, types, constraints, outputs, error semantics, and compatibility. Compare the contract with the sequence and each case; do not invent status codes or schemas.
- **Architecture:** Compare a simple modular design, layered/onion style, and event-driven or distributed options where relevant. Consider change isolation, dependency direction, testability, team familiarity, operational cost, consistency, deployment independence, and complexity. Onion architecture places domain policy inward and external mechanisms outward, with dependencies pointing inward; extra abstractions may be unnecessary for a small system. A modular monolith can simplify deployment but shares a process and release lifecycle. Asynchronous messaging can decouple time and smooth load, but adds delivery, ordering, observability, and consistency concerns. These are tradeoffs, not guarantees.
- **Infrastructure and failures:** For actual dependencies identify timeouts, capacity, failure and recovery behavior, transaction and consistency constraints. Consider duplicate delivery, concurrent updates, partial completion, idempotency, retry limits, backoff, and visibility when relevant. Ask which outcomes are acceptable before choosing mechanisms. Verify provider-specific behavior in current official documentation.
- **Boundaries and models:** Draw responsibilities and dependency direction before defining interfaces. Specify caller, operation, input/output, ownership, errors, and invariants. Keep business models, transport DTOs and storage records distinct only where differences justify it. Avoid interfaces without a concrete boundary or need.
- **Technology:** Compare viable candidates against constraints, skills, deployment and operational burden, maintenance, and migration. Record evidence and unknowns; choose after the human evaluates tradeoffs.
- **Tests:** Map case IDs and failure scenarios to observable outcomes. Choose unit, integration, contract, and end-to-end tests where they offer useful confidence. Include negative inputs, boundaries, authorization, concurrency, retries, and failures when relevant. State what is real or simulated and the risk each test covers.
